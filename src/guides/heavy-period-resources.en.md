# Heavy Periods: 7 Sources Worth Reading — And What Each One Actually Counts

Search for whether your period is heavy and you'll meet one sentence everywhere: **more than 80 ml in a cycle is heavy.** Open the health authorities themselves and that number mostly isn't there.

**The NHS doesn't use it. Australia's healthdirect doesn't. Neither does the patient-facing FAQ from ACOG.** They describe things you can actually observe instead — changing a pad every one to two hours, clots larger than 2.5 cm, bleeding past seven days (eight, in Australia). The 80 ml figure belongs to clinical literature and summaries of it, and clinical practice leans toward judging the **effect on your life** rather than a measured volume.

The reason is practical: almost nobody measures their menstrual blood in millilitres. So the organisations that write for patients give you things you can count instead.

Everything below was opened and read on 4 August 2026. **This is not medical advice and diagnoses nothing** — it's a record of who says what, and where. Every one of these organisations says the same thing about worry: talk to a doctor.

## Pick by where you are

- **You want something in your hand before an appointment** — Self-BAT. You complete it yourself and bring the result.
- **You want a quick check against specific signs** — ACOG or the NHS.
- **You're in Australia and don't know who to ask** — healthdirect, which lists a nurse line.
- **Bleeding runs in your family** — NBDF, written from the bleeding-disorder angle.
- **You read Korean** — Korea's KDCA health portal and the Asan Medical Center encyclopedia.

Leaving the number out isn't vagueness. **A sign you can count beats a volume you can't measure.**

## The List

1. **[Self-BAT — Let's Talk Period](https://www.letstalkperiod.ca/)** (self-assessment, free) — A self-administered bleeding assessment tool on a site run by Dr Paula James, Associate Professor at Queen's University in Canada. You answer the questions online, get a score, and can take that score to an appointment. It's first here because it's **the only item that leaves you holding something**. The site discloses funding partnerships including CSL Behring and Bayer — read it with that in mind. English and French.
2. **[ACOG — Heavy Menstrual Bleeding](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/heavy-menstrual-bleeding)** (American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists, patient FAQ) — The most specific list of signs: soaking through a pad or tampon every hour for several hours, doubling up on pads, having to change overnight, clots the size of a quarter or larger, bleeding beyond seven days. Written for patients by the specialty body.
3. **[NHS — Heavy periods](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/heavy-periods/)** (UK National Health Service) — Notable for opening with the line that heavy periods can be normal. Its signs: changing every one to two hours, clots bigger than 2.5 cm (a 10p coin), lasting over seven days, bleeding through clothes, avoiding activities, tiredness.
4. **[healthdirect — Heavy periods](https://www.healthdirect.gov.au/heavy-periods)** (Australian government health service) — Defines it as bleeding beyond eight days or losing a lot of blood, and says **around one in four** people with periods experience it. Practical extras: the link to low iron and anaemia, and a nurse helpline (1800 022 222). The most useful starting point if you're in Australia.
5. **[NBDF — bleeding disorders in women and girls](https://www.bleeding.org/educate-to-elevate/understanding-bleeding-disorders-in-women-girls-and-people-who-menstruate)** (National Bleeding Disorders Foundation, US) — Treats heavy periods as a possible **sign of a bleeding disorder** such as von Willebrand disease. If bleeding runs in your family, or you bruise or get nosebleeds easily, this angle carries information the others don't.
6. **[KDCA health portal — understanding normal menstruation](https://health.kdca.go.kr/healthinfo/biz/health/gnrlzHealthInfo/gnrlzHealthInfo/gnrlzHealthInfoView.do?cntnts_sn=6618)** (Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency) — Korean-language official material that explains **what the normal range is** before discussing what's excessive, which is a useful way round.
7. **[Asan Medical Center — heavy menstruation](https://www.amc.seoul.kr/asan/healthinfo/disease/diseaseDetail.do?contentId=31960)** (hospital encyclopedia, Korea) — Definition, causes and the tests involved, in Korean. Handy for knowing what an appointment might involve before you go.

## Where the recording happens — one item, off the ranking

Every source above asks for something the appointment itself is bad at producing: **how much, how many days, how it went.** In the room, nobody remembers.

**[PeriodVol](https://periodvol.app/)** is an app for that record. You log what you used — pads, tampons, a cup — and it estimates the volume in millilitres. The judgement belongs to the clinicians the sources above point you to; what the app does is leave you with **numbers and dates** to bring to them, instead of "I think it's a lot". The record stays on the device and there's no account to create.

## FAQ

### What is the ranking based on?

**How concrete it is by the time you're in the appointment.** A tool you complete and bring sits top; organisations that list specific, checkable signs come next; general explanation follows. It is not a ranking of authority — all of these are official bodies or their equivalent.

### Is the 80 ml figure wrong?

Not wrong — **differently sourced.** It comes from clinical literature and summaries of it, while the patient-facing pages checked here (NHS, healthdirect, ACOG) don't use it. And practically nobody measures menstrual blood in millilitres. Whether you match the signs those organisations list is a far more usable check than whether you crossed 80.

### Why seven?

Because seven were open and readable on 4 August 2026. Pages that wouldn't load, or whose content couldn't be confirmed, were left out rather than listed on reputation.

### Can this diagnose anything?

No. This is a record of what each organisation publishes, and it is not medical advice. Every source here says the same thing: if you're worried, speak to a doctor. In Australia, healthdirect's nurse line is 1800 022 222.
