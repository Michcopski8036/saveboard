# Flo & Clue Alternatives: 6 Apps, and What You Give Up

Two people go looking for the same thing and mean completely different things by it.

One opens her tracker in the waiting room, because the question on the way in was "are your periods heavy?" — and what the app holds is three cycles of *medium*. That answers nothing, and she wants an app that produces a number.

The other has just read that a federal jury found Meta liable for collecting Flo users' data, and that is reason enough on its own.

Those two people should not download the same replacement. Most "Flo alternatives" lists are written as if there were only one reason to leave — privacy — and rank apps against that single axis. This one splits the reasons apart, and says out loud what each switch costs you, because every one of them costs something.

## Four different reasons to leave

- **Privacy** — you want the data on your device, and ideally in code someone outside the company can read.
- **Subscription fatigue** — the free tier keeps shrinking and the paywall keeps moving.
- **Feature bloat** — you wanted a calendar and got a content platform, a community, and a pregnancy plan.
- **No measurement** — you can log *light / medium / heavy*, but nothing in the app turns that into a figure you can take to an appointment.

The first three are well served by the list below. The fourth is a different problem, and it sits in its own section further down, off the ranking.

## How this was checked

Every rating count, star average and update date below was read off the **US App Store on 11 August 2026** — except Mensinator, which isn't on the App Store; its version and date come from its F-Droid page, read the same day. Privacy scores come from the Mozilla Foundation's [review of period and ovulation trackers](https://www.mozillafoundation.org/en/nothing-personal/period-ovulation-trackers/), published 16 July 2026, in which six apps were tested hands-on. Open-source versions and licences were read off [F-Droid](https://f-droid.org/) and the projects' own sites.

Two numbers are worth more than the star average: **how many people have rated an app**, and **when it last shipped an update**. A 4.18 from 147 people and a 4.75 from 1.9 million are not the same measurement. An app that hasn't been updated in eleven months is a different proposition from one that shipped yesterday, however good its privacy policy is.

## About the jury verdict — precisely what happened

This is the fact doing most of the work in the "switch away from Flo" posts, and it is usually repeated second-hand, so here it is from the court file.

In *Frasco v. Flo Health, Inc.*, No. 21-cv-00757-JD in the Northern District of California, the [verdict form](https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.cand.372884/gov.uscourts.cand.372884.756.0_2.pdf) signed by the presiding juror on 1 August 2025 and entered on the [docket](https://www.courtlistener.com/docket/55370837/frasco-v-flo-health-inc/) on 4 August asks three questions under the California Invasion of Privacy Act. The jury answered **yes**, Meta intentionally eavesdropped on or recorded plaintiffs' conversations using an electronic device; **yes**, they had a reasonable expectation it was not being overheard or recorded; and **no**, Meta did not have the consent of all parties.

Two things that get lost in the retelling. The questions the jury answered were about **Meta**, not about Flo. And the conduct at issue is historical: the class period in this case runs **from 2016 to 2019**. It is a real fact about how this data has been handled, and it is not a description of the app you would install today.

Two more things, for accuracy. This is a **jury verdict, not a final judgment** — Meta moved to decertify the class on 11 August 2025, and no final judgment or appeal appears on the docket as of 11 August 2026. And separately from the Meta trial, the class settlements with Flo Health and Google received preliminary approval on 22 April 2026, with a fairness hearing set for 29 October 2026.

## The two you're leaving — a fair word

Flo and Clue lead this category, and the numbers say why. Flo carries **1,941,328 ratings at 4.75** and Clue **401,033 at 4.76**; both shipped an update in the first week of August 2026. Nothing below has that many people testing it. Flo's own listing describes an Anonymous Mode that lets you use it without a name or email; Clue is Berlin-based and points at GDPR, tracks 200+ symptoms, and scores **8/10** with Mozilla — the highest of any mainstream app in the review.

If what you want is prediction that has been tuned on an enormous number of cycles, a large content library, or pregnancy and perimenopause coverage in the same app, staying put is a perfectly defensible answer. Leave for a reason, not for a mood.

## The List

1. **[drip](https://dripapp.org/)** (open source under GPL-3.0, free) — Built by Heart of Code e.V., a Berlin non-profit, with everything kept on the device and the source published on GitLab, so the privacy claim is one you can check rather than one you have to accept. It supports the symptothermal method for fertility awareness, and deliberately isn't "another cute, pink app". The cost of switching is scale: **56 ratings at 4.04** on the US App Store, and the last release was 14 January 2026. Its Mastodon account has 619 followers and 94 posts in total — this is a small volunteer project, and you should choose it knowing that.
2. **[Euki](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/euki/id1469213846)** (free, no in-app purchases) — The **highest privacy score in Mozilla's entire review, 10/10.** A non-profit, open-source project; the listing describes a customisable PIN, data stored locally and nowhere else, and reproductive-health information alongside the tracking. The thing you must weigh against that: **the last release was 4 September 2025** — close to a year without an update as of writing. Nothing is broken by that on its own, but an unmaintained app eventually meets an OS version that disagrees with it.
3. **[Mensinator](https://f-droid.org/en/packages/com.mensinator.app/)** (open source under MIT, Android only) — The most practical answer to "I want to leave but not lose four years of logs": version 2.1, released 31 July 2026, added **importing from Flo and Clue**. No sign-up, no cloud, data on the device, and a home-screen widget. Two limits, stated plainly: **Android only**, and distribution is through F-Droid rather than an app store most people already have open.
4. **[Period Calendar](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/period-tracker-period-calendar/id896501514)** (free download, iOS and Android) — The mainstream option on this list, and the highest-rated app in the table at **4.92 from 172,515 ratings**, updated 7 August 2026. Its listing says no registration is needed and that data stays on the device. Mozilla still scored it **6/10** — below Clue, above Flo. If the switch is about wanting fewer moving parts rather than a privacy audit, this is the softest landing here.
5. **[Spot On](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/spot-on-period-tracker/id1039914781)** (Planned Parenthood Direct, free) — Built around **birth control** first: method-specific reminders and guidance, with period and fertile-window prediction alongside. **15,405 ratings at 4.25**, last updated 2 February 2026, Mozilla **5/10**. Worth a look if your reason for leaving is that your app buried contraception under everything else — and note that the star average is the lowest of the mainstream apps here.
6. **[Stardust](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/stardust-period-tracker/id1495829322)** (free download, iOS and Android) — Included because it is genuinely where a lot of people go: **113,664 ratings at 4.76**, and it shipped an update on 10 August 2026, the most recent of anything here. Its listing describes blending science, "ancient wisdom", astronomy and AI. But it scored **2/10 with Mozilla, the lowest in that review** — so if privacy is your reason for leaving Flo, moving here moves you in the wrong direction. Choose it for the experience, with the score in front of you.

If your reason for leaving was privacy, the first three are the honest answers, and each one asks you to accept a smaller, slower app in return. If your reason was fatigue or clutter, the last three keep the polish.

## Where the millilitres are — one item, off the ranking

The fourth reason at the top of this page — *no measurement* — isn't solved by anything above. Every app on this list, Flo and Clue included, records flow as **light, medium or heavy**. Millilitres have historically lived in clinical tools like the PBAC, not in consumer apps, and the number usually quoted as the heavy-bleeding line is **80 ml per cycle**.

**[PeriodVol](https://periodvol.app/)** exists for that one gap. You tap the picture of what you used — pad, tampon or cup — and how soaked it was, and it estimates the volume in millilitres per day and per cycle, then flags a cycle that crosses 80 ml. The estimates and their limits are written up on its [methodology page](https://periodvol.app/methodology), which is worth reading before you trust any figure it gives you. The app is **free to use, with one optional one-time purchase** — US$2.99, not a subscription.

Three honest caveats, because this list has been about trade-offs throughout. **PeriodVol is not a medical device and diagnoses nothing** — 80 ml is a reference line, and what it does is leave you with numbers and dates for the appointment instead of "I think it's a lot". It is **closed source and paid**, so on the privacy axis specifically, drip and Euki beat it — their code can be read and PeriodVol's cannot. And an estimate from what you used is an estimate; a menstrual cup with markings on it is more accurate than any app.

If it's the appointment you're preparing for, our guide to [what the health authorities actually count as a heavy period](/guides/heavy-period-resources) lines up which measure each organisation leads with — signs, duration, volume — and is more useful than any app comparison.

## FAQ

### What is the ranking based on?

**How verifiable the switch is, then how well maintained the app is.** Open-source, local-first apps whose claims anyone can check sit at the top; mainstream apps follow, ordered by their Mozilla privacy score. It is not a ranking of prediction accuracy — we did not run the same cycle history through all six and compare the forecasts.

### Why aren't Flo and Clue in the list?

Because they're what you're leaving, so ranking them as alternatives to themselves would be silly. They're in the table as the baseline you're measuring against, and they're both strong: between them they hold over 2.3 million App Store ratings, and Clue's 8/10 is the highest privacy score of any mainstream app in Mozilla's review.

### Is Euki safe to use if it hasn't been updated?

Nothing in an app breaks on a schedule, and its privacy design doesn't decay. But **4 September 2025** is a long time between releases, and unmaintained apps eventually collide with an OS update, a changed permission model, or a bug nobody is left to fix. That's information, not a verdict — you're entitled to have it before you move a year of logs.

### Does any of this diagnose anything?

No. This page compares apps, not health. Every app here, and this guide, is not a medical device and not medical advice. If your bleeding worries you, that conversation belongs with a doctor — and the more specific your record, the shorter the conversation.

### How current is this?

App Store figures were read on 11 August 2026, and the Mozilla scores come from a review published on 16 July 2026. Rating counts move daily and privacy policies change without notice; check the listing before you commit anything personal to an app.
