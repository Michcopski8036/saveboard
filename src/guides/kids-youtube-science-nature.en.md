# Kids Science YouTube: Every Link Opened

Search for Science Max, the big-experiments show that turns up on nearly every list of science channels for kids, and the address most people try first is sciencemax.com. On 5 September 2026 that domain returned eleven lines of HTML — the words “Coming soon …” under a logo, and a meta description reading “ScienceMax.com - Quality STEAM Supplies”. It is a shop that has not opened. The show’s own site is at sciencemax.ca, and it is one screen with two buttons on it: Season 1 and Season 2.

Nobody needs another list of recommended science channels; there are hundreds, and most of them copy each other. So this is not one. We took the channels that keep circulating on those lists and opened each one’s official site to see whether the thing being recommended still checks out.

## What we could check, and what we could not

YouTube blocks the fetcher used for this guide. Channel pages come back as an empty JavaScript shell with no body text, and `youtube.com/feeds/videos.xml` returns HTTP 404 for both the modern `channel_id` parameter and the retired `user` one. A control request to another site with the same query-string shape returned normally, so this is YouTube refusing, not a broken tool.

Three consequences, stated up front:

- **There are no subscriber counts, video counts or last-upload dates in this guide.** We could not see them, and we are not going to estimate them. If you need to know whether a channel is still posting, open it yourself — that takes about ten seconds and is more reliable than any number we could have written here.
- **Every channel URL below is one an official site links to.** Guessing handles is genuinely dangerous: `youtube.com/@LittleFox` does not belong to the Korean reading service everyone means by that name — it answers as a Chinese-language channel called 妖狐大小姐. A parent who follows a guessed handle lands somewhere nobody vetted.
- **Everything else — who makes it, what country, what ages, what is free** — was read off the official site on 5 September 2026 and is quoted where it matters. Where a site does not say, this guide says it does not say.

## Each one, in detail

1. **[SciShow Kids](https://www.youtube.com/user/scishowkids)** (Complexly · nonprofit · no age stated) — The subscribe link on Complexly’s own page for the show is where this URL comes from. Complexly describes itself as “a nonprofit that creates thoughtful, well-researched educational content”, and says it “started a new chapter by becoming a 501(c)(3) nonprofit” in early 2026 — the same organisation behind Crash Course and SciShow, started in 2012 by Hank and John Green. One oddity worth knowing: the show’s page on complexly.org is still the launch announcement. It is dated 23 February 2015 and reads “SciShow Kids, hosted by Jessi Knudsen Castañeda, premieres in March.” That tells you the page is old. It tells you nothing about whether the channel posted this week — those are two different facts and lists routinely mix them up.
2. **[Crash Course Kids](https://www.youtube.com/crashcoursekids)** (Complexly · states 3rd to 5th grade) — The rarest thing in this whole guide: a channel whose own people state an age. Complexly’s page reads, word for word, “this channel for 3rd- to 5th-graders engages learners’ curiosity with STEM and humanities subjects”, and “Join us for standards-matched videos to support any classroom or homeschool space.” If your child is four, this is not the one, whatever the round-ups say. The URL comes from a Crash Course video description republished on complexly.org, which lists it as “CC Kids”.
3. **[National Geographic](https://www.youtube.com/@NatGeo)** (the general channel, not a kids channel) — This entry is here because of what is missing. Every list recommends *Nat Geo Kids*. We could not find an official Nat Geo property that links a Kids YouTube channel: kids.nationalgeographic.com links none, and natgeokids.com links Facebook and Instagram only. The one YouTube URL National Geographic’s own site publishes is `@NatGeo`, the general-audience channel. So that is what we link, labelled for what it is. Publisher named on the site: National Geographic Partners, LLC. The site does not state a country or an age range.

## The two we left out, and exactly why

**Science Max.** The official site is sciencemax.ca and it links no YouTube channel, names no producer, states no age and offers Season 1 and Season 2 only. Meanwhile, searching the show surfaces two different YouTube channels using the name — one titled “Science Max: Experiments at Large!” and another titled “Science Max - Scholastic”. With no official source telling us which is the show’s own, linking either would be a coin flip we are asking a parent to take. And sciencemax.com, the address most people type, is that unopened STEAM supply shop.

**National Geographic Kids**, as covered above: the brand is real and the magazine subscription is real, but no official page we could reach links a Kids YouTube channel, so we do not publish a URL for one.

## What “ad-free” actually describes

This is the part the round-ups get wrong most often, and it is worth being precise, because the sloppy version of it is false.

**YouTube itself is free.** Anyone can watch every channel above without paying anything. Nothing here is a hidden paywall.

What is true is narrower: the phrases these lists quote as channel features are usually taken from a *different product* on the same company’s website. When a site says “completely ad-free”, it is describing its own subscription player — not YouTube, where ads run for everyone without a Premium account. A list that reprints “ad-free” next to a YouTube link has moved a sentence from one product to another.

The nonprofit version of the same gap is worth naming too. Complexly’s Partner With Us page invites sponsors to “reach millions of engaged lifelong learners through sponsorship placements on our shows”. That is a sponsor read inside the video, which exists independently of whatever YouTube serves around it. Neither fact is a scandal. Both are things a parent may reasonably want to know before deciding what plays on the tablet.

## Keeping what you actually found

The URL is the least valuable thing on this page. The valuable part is the sentence attached to it — *this one states 3rd to 5th grade*, *this one is the general channel not the kids one*, *we could not confirm this one at all*. ## FAQ

### Why are there no subscriber counts or upload dates?

Because we could not see them. YouTube returns an empty shell to the fetcher used here and 404s its own RSS feed endpoint, so any number in those columns would have been a guess dressed as a fact. Most round-ups print them anyway. One of the ones we read claims its channels were “re-checked for active status and new uploads” in a footer dated two months *earlier* than the data date in its own body text.

### Are the recommended ages in other guides wrong?

Not necessarily wrong — mostly just unsourced. Of everything checked for this guide, only Crash Course Kids states an age band itself, and it is 3rd to 5th grade. When you see “ages 4–7” attached to a channel in a list, it is usually the writer’s estimate, not the channel’s claim, and it is not labelled as either.

### Is any of this a paid recommendation?

No. Nobody paid to appear here, and two entries are here because they failed the check rather than passed it. The SaveBoard card above the list is our own product and is labelled as an advertisement — that is the only commercial thing on this page.

### How current is this?

Every site was opened on 5 September 2026 and the quotes are from that day. Domains move: the address a list published in 2023 may now be a parking page, a shop, or someone else entirely. If you are reading this much later, the checks are worth redoing rather than trusting.
